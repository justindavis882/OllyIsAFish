The Threshold Language: A Foundation Guide

Introduction

This guide contains the structural foundation of our family language. It is designed to be spoken natively by children, seamlessly adopted by adult friends, and strictly enforced at our front door.

The language operates on a flawless, exception-free logic system: words are built from simple consonant-vowel pairs, verbs never change their root forms, and vocabulary is constructed from practical, everyday use.

Note on Proper Names:

Proper names such as Olly, Karen, George, and Zola—bypass all language rules and are spoken exactly as they are in English.

Chapter 1: The Engine (Phonetics & Grammar)

1.1 The Alphabet & Pronunciation

The language uses a simplified alphabet designed to eliminate spelling confusion and ensure accessibility for developing speakers.

  • The 5 Universal Vowels: A (ah), E (eh), I (ee), O (oh), U (oo).
  • The 15 Consonants: B, D, F, H, J, K, M, N, P, R, S, T, V, W, Y.
The Golden Rule of Speech: Every word must follow a strict CV (Consonant-Vowel) structure. Consonants never touch each other, and words never end in a consonant. The only exception is the word A (and).

1.2 Sentence Structure

Sentences are built using the Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) order.

  • Rule: The actor comes first, the action comes second, the target comes third.
    Example: Olly wiyo titi. (Olly sees the baby.)
  • Possession: Place pronouns directly after the noun.
    • Doma nu (Our house).
    • Bata tu (Your shoe).

1.3 The Verb Engine (Time)

Verbs have no irregular forms. Time is indicated by adding a two-letter suffix to the root action.

  • Present (Happening now): Root verb. → Moku (Eat).
  • Past (Already happened): Add -pa. → Mokupa (Ate).
  • Future (Will happen): Add -to. → Mokuto (Will eat).

1.4 Plurals & Reduplication

To indicate more than one of an object, simply double the word (reduplication).

  • Kobi (Cat) → Kobikobi (Cats)
  • Kupa (Cup) → Kupakupa (Cups)

1.5 Negation & Connectors

  • Negation: Place Na immediately before the verb. → Mi na kute. (I do not enter.)
  • Connectors: Use the single vowel A for "and." → Bata a koto. (Shoe and coat.)

1.6 Asking Questions

Questions are formed without changing the sentence order (in-situ).

  • Yes/No Questions: Add Ke to the very end of the sentence.
    Joseph kute ke? (Is Joseph entering?)
  • W-Questions: Replace the target object with a Ke- word.
    • Words: Kemu (Who), Keka (What), Kera (Where), Keti (When), Kemo (Why), Kesu (How).
    Example: Tu mokupa keka? (You ate what?)

Chapter 3: Numerals & Counting

The number system is built on unique CV syllables to allow for rapid counting.

Building Larger Numbers

To create larger numbers, simply combine the place values exactly as they are spoken.

  • Tens: Combine the multiplier with Da (10).
    • 20 = Duda (Two-Ten)
    • 30 = Tida (Three-Ten)
  • Compound Numbers:
    • 24 = Duda-Ko (Two-Ten Four)
    • 57 = Mada-Re (Five-Ten Seven)
  • Hundreds: The word for 100 is Huna.
    • 100 = Pa-huna
    • 350 = Ti-huna Mada
Example Phrase: Vutu du kupa mi. (Give me two cups.)

Chapter 4: Time, Dates & Weather

This vocabulary governs how we describe the world outside the threshold.

Cycles of Time

By combining the root word for Day (Deya) with our tense markers (Pa/Nawa/To), we logically derive the days of the week.

  • Deya: Day
  • Nati: Night
  • Wika: Week
  • Muna: Month
  • Yera: Year
  • Nade: Today (Nawa + Deya)
  • Pade: Yesterday (Pa + Deya)
  • Tode: Tomorrow (To + Deya)

The Weather (Wida)

  • Sona: Sun / Sunny
  • Reni: Rain / Raining
  • Niva: Snow / Snowing
  • Kudo: Cloud / Cloudy
  • Wida: Wind / Weather

Example Phrases:

  • Wida hina Blackwood budi nade. (The weather here in Blackwood is bad today.)
  • Tode, sona rapi. (Tomorrow, the sun is good.)
  • Piri kute doma, reni tora. (Please enter the house, it is raining there.)
  • Nu puyepa titi pade. (We played with the baby yesterday.)

Putting it into Practice (The Kitchen Flow)

Because you have the grammar engine fully installed, your friends and family can now navigate making dinner, feeding the cats, and cleaning up—all without breaking the threshold rule.

Dinner Prep:

  • Suwa, piri tiko veta a mito.(Joseph, please cut the vegetables and meat.)
  • Mi satoto fudo nawa.(I will cook the food now.)
  • Biro wata kura.(Stir the cold water.)

Eating:

  • Miya fami! Moku fudo.(Welcome family! Eat the food.)
  • Vutu peta a foti titi.(Give a plate and fork to the baby.)
  • Bomi hina suta ke?(Is the bread here sweet?)

Cleaning Up:

  • Kemu kafito kupakupa?(Who will wash the cups?)
  • Piri vutu fudo budi ruso.(Please give the bad food to the trash.)

Modifier Stacking Principles

Because descriptor terms behave identically to ownership markers, multiple attributes attach sequentially without compromising structural logic.

  • Kobi dako mi → Cat dark my (My black cat)
  • Kupa bika buru → Vessel large blue (The large blue cup)

Practical Applications (Descriptive Phrases)

Positioning modifiers directly after their target allows the speaker to form rich, nuanced descriptions instantly.

Guiding Young Speakers:

  • Wiyo keka? Bata redo!(Look at what? A red footwear!)
  • Titi puyeto sepa rona.(The infant will interact with the circular object.)
  • Kobi dako fasa!(The dark feline moves quickly!)
  • Piri vutu fura yero titi.(Kindly offer the yellow produce to the child.)

Locating Items:

  • Mi hika musi nuwa.(I listen to modern songs.)
  • Piri kafi peta doti.(Kindly sanitize the soiled dish.)
  • Suwa, bako hina hevi.(Partner, the sack here carries weight.)
  • Tebu boka tora.(The square surface sits over there.)

Sharing Perspectives:

  • Beda nu sofi a rapi.(Our sleeping area feels plush and pleasant.)
  • Kema hina hada.(This activity presents a challenge.)
  • Mabo, wida kura a dako nawa.(Friend, conditions are chilly and dim currently.)

  • Woka: Work / Job
  • Suku: School
  • Siti: City / Town
  • Paka: Park / Nature
  • Roda: Road / Street

3. Directions & Navigation (Weya)

Essential tools when guiding a driver or assisting a child with spatial understanding!

  • Weya: Way / Path / Direction
  • Refa: Left
  • Rata: Right
  • Tupa: Up / Top
  • Dona: Down
  • Fona: Front / Forward
  • Baka: Back / Behind (Note: Bako indicates Container, whereas Baka designates Rear!)
  • Nira: Near / Close
  • Fara: Far

4. Travel Actions (Verbs)

Note: The system already incorporates Wako (Walk/Go) alongside Hato (Stop/Wait).

  • Mova: Move / Drive
  • Tona: Turn
  • Riva: Arrive
  • Viwa: Leave / Depart (Adapted from View/Away... alternatively, Depa is used).
  • Depa: Depart / Leave

📍 The "Nesting" Trick for Locations

By leveraging our regular grammar system, speakers can stack positional terms to pinpoint exact locations without relying on extra prepositions (such as in, on, at).

  • In the car: Hina mota (Here vehicle)
  • On the table: Tupa tebu (Top table)
  • Under the bed: Dona beda (Down bed)
  • Outside the house: Ata doma (Outside home)

Putting it into Practice (The Travel Flow)

Armed with these core terms, conversation transitions seamlessly from inside the house to the road.

In the Car / Navigating:

  • Suwa, tona refa hina.(Joseph, turn left here.)
  • Sopa bika nira ke?(Is the large shop nearby?)
  • Mova mota fasa!(Drive the vehicle rapidly!)
  • Nu hato roda redo.(We stop at the red signal.)
  • Tona rata, woka tora.(Turn right, work sits over there.)

Running Errands & Plans:

  • Nu wakoto paka nade.(We will head to the park today.)
  • Kemu depato suku keti?(Who leaves school at what time?)
  • Mi movato mota Siti.(I will drive the car into town.)
  • Titi wiyopa basa yero.(The infant spotted the yellow bus.)

Spatial Awareness:

  • Wiyo tupa! Pena bika tora.(Look above! A large aircraft sits up there.)
  • Titi, wako fona.(Child, step forward.)
  • Bako tu baka sepa.(Your container rests behind the object.)

Chapter 10: Health & Safety (Heta a Safi)

1. Medical & Body (Heta)

  • Heta: Health
  • Sika: Sick / Ill
  • Bubu: Injury / Wound / "Boo-boo"
  • Bura: Blood
  • Mesi: Medicine / Pill
  • Doka: Doctor
  • Bada: Bandage / Band-aid
  • Hura: Hurt / Pain (from Ch. 6)

2. Safety & Alerts (Safi)

  • Safi: Safe / Safety (from Ch. 2)
  • Daja: Danger / Dangerous (Dan-ger → Da-ja)
  • Hepa: Help
  • Kero: Careful / Caution (Care)
  • Kori: Fire (from Ch. 5)
  • Hoka: Hot (from Ch. 2)

3. Actions & Reactions

  • Foda: Fall / Drop (Fall-down → Fo-da)
  • Bero: Break / Broken
  • Kira: Cry
  • Biri: Breathe
  • Hato: Stop (from Ch. 2 - an essential protective action!)

🚑 The Power of Compound Safety Words

By combining Sika and Doma to form "Hospital," visitors can rapidly construct logical expressions during urgent moments using established roots:

  • Fire Truck: Kori-mota (Fire vehicle)
  • Ambulance: Hepa-mota (Help vehicle) or Sika-mota (Sick vehicle)
  • First-aid kit: Heta-bako (Health container)

Putting it into Practice (The Safety Flow)

Equipped with this toolkit, household members can address scraped joints or kitchen hazards entirely within the language rules.

Tumbles & Injuries:

  • Titi fodapa!(The baby collapsed!)
  • Titi, tu hura ke?(Child, do you feel pain?)
  • Oh na, bubu tora. Bura hina.(Oh no, a harm over there. Blood sits here.)
  • Piri vutu bada a mesi mi.(Kindly pass a bandage and remedy to me.)
  • Na kira, titi. Tu safi nawa.(Avoid weeping, little one. You remain secure currently.)

Hazards & Warnings:

  • Hato! Tebu hina daja.(Wait! The table here presents danger.)
  • Kero, kupa hoka peni.(Caution, the container stays extremely warm.)
  • Na kute wataduka, toya beropa.(Do not step inside the washroom, the toilet broke.)
  • Suwa, wiyo titi. Bako daja tora.(Partner, observe the infant. The container there carries risk.)

Requesting Support:

  • Hepa! Mi sika nade.(Assistance! I feel unwell today.)
  • Hedo mi hura. Mi takato mesi.(My head hurts. I shall consume remedy.)
  • Nu movato sikadoma.(We shall drive to the medical facility.)
